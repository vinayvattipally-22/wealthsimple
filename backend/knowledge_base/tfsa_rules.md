# Tax-Free Savings Account (TFSA) Rules
Source: CRA Publication RC4466 — Tax-Free Savings Account (TFSA) Guide for Individuals
Last Updated: 2024 Tax Year

## What is a TFSA?
A Tax-Free Savings Account (TFSA) is a registered account that allows Canadian residents aged 18 or older to earn investment income — including interest, dividends, and capital gains — completely tax-free. Contributions are not tax-deductible (made with after-tax dollars), but all growth and withdrawals are tax-free.

## Eligibility
- Must be a Canadian resident with a valid Social Insurance Number (SIN).
- Must be 18 years of age or older.
- Contribution room accumulates from the year you turn 18 (or from 2009, whichever is later), even if you don't open a TFSA.
- Non-residents can hold a TFSA but cannot accumulate new contribution room while non-resident, and contributions made while non-resident are subject to a 1% per month tax.

## TFSA Annual Contribution Limits
| Year(s) | Annual Limit |
|---------|-------------|
| 2009–2012 | $5,000 |
| 2013–2014 | $5,500 |
| 2015 | $10,000 |
| 2016–2018 | $5,500 |
| 2019–2022 | $6,000 |
| 2023 | $6,500 |
| 2024 | $7,000 |
| 2025 | $7,000 |

## Cumulative Contribution Room
- For someone who was 18+ and a Canadian resident since 2009: the cumulative room through 2024 is $95,000.
- Through 2025: $102,000.
- Unused contribution room carries forward indefinitely.

## Withdrawal and Recontribution Rules
- Withdrawals from a TFSA are completely tax-free and are not considered taxable income.
- Withdrawals do NOT affect eligibility for income-tested benefits (OAS, GIS, GST/HST credit, Canada Child Benefit, etc.).
- Withdrawn amounts are added back to contribution room, but only in the following calendar year.
- Important: If you withdraw $5,000 in 2024, you can recontribute that $5,000 starting January 1, 2025 — NOT in 2024. Recontributing in the same year uses up current-year room and may cause an overcontribution.

## Overcontribution Penalty
- Contributions exceeding the available TFSA room are subject to a penalty tax of 1% per month on the highest excess amount in each month.
- There is NO $2,000 grace amount (unlike RRSPs).
- The penalty applies until the excess is withdrawn or absorbed by new contribution room in January.
- CRA Form RC243 (Tax-Free Savings Account (TFSA) Return) must be filed.

## Qualifying Investments
TFSAs can hold the same types of investments as RRSPs:
- Cash and savings deposits
- GICs
- Government and corporate bonds
- Mutual funds and ETFs
- Publicly traded securities on a designated stock exchange
- Certain small business shares (with restrictions)
- Non-qualified or prohibited investments trigger special taxes.

## TFSA on Death
- If a successor holder (spouse or common-law partner) is designated, the TFSA continues in their name with no tax consequences. The value becomes part of the survivor's TFSA without affecting their contribution room.
- If a beneficiary (not successor holder) is designated, the fair market value at date of death is received tax-free. Any growth between date of death and distribution is taxable to the beneficiary.
- If no successor holder or beneficiary is named, the TFSA value flows through the estate.

## TFSA vs RRSP Decision Factors
| Factor | TFSA Preferred | RRSP Preferred |
|--------|---------------|----------------|
| Current tax bracket | Low bracket now | High bracket now |
| Expected retirement bracket | Higher in retirement | Lower in retirement |
| Need for flexible access | Yes — no tax on withdrawal | No — penalty-free only at retirement |
| Income-tested benefits | Protecting GIS/OAS eligibility | Not a concern |
| Home purchase | Already used HBP or ineligible | First-time buyer (HBP available) |

## Key Tax Planning Strategies with TFSAs
1. Maximize TFSA contributions before non-registered investments — all growth is permanently tax-free.
2. Hold high-growth investments (equities, ETFs) in TFSA to maximize the tax-free benefit.
3. Hold interest-bearing investments (GICs, bonds) in RRSP instead — interest is taxed at the highest marginal rate.
4. Use TFSA for emergency fund — accessible anytime without tax consequences.
5. In retirement, use TFSA withdrawals to supplement income without triggering OAS clawback.
6. For low-income earners, TFSA is generally preferred over RRSP since the RRSP deduction has less value and RRSP withdrawals may reduce government benefits.
7. Track withdrawal timing carefully — recontribution room is only restored the following January.
